\begin{PRSabstract}
Instrument size constrains visualization and manoeuvrability during transoral robotic surgery (TORS). This paper demonstrates feasibility of TORS with miniature 3 mm wristed instruments and provides a comparison with standard 8 mm instruments through simulated cleft palate repairs in live porcine models. Procedures were performed by one surgeon with analysis including three 3 mm and four 8 mm instrument trials. Endoscopic video was analyzed to quantify differences in visual field obstruction, procedure duration, and suture timings. The median of visual field obstruction was 10.4\% with 3 mm instruments and 23.9\% with 8 mm instruments. Across 62 stitches, the mean suture placement times were 7.1 versus 4.1 minutes, measured from needle pickup to tightening of the final throw, and the mean robotic procedure duration was \(121.5 \pm 24.8\) versus \(81.3 \pm 22.0\) minutes (mean \(\pm\) SD) for the 3 mm and 8 mm instruments respectively. \verifytext{\textbf{[Repair completion and closure quality: pending expert review.]}} The smaller instruments decrease visual field obstruction but on average present slightly longer suturing times. Specific quantitative interpretation is limited by the small population size of the surgeon and models, as well as inconsistencies in operative steps and tool usage, which does not disprove feasibility. Despite performance differences in this specific experimental setup, the miniaturization of tools maintains equivalent robotic dexterity and opens the door for greater innovative intervention.

\PRSkeywords{transoral robotic surgery; cleft palate repair; minimally invasive robot assisted surgery; porcine model}
\end{PRSabstract}

\section*{Miniature Instruments for Simulated Cleft Palate Repair}
Cleft palate repair requires delicate tissue handling within the limited space of the oral cavity \cite{podolsky2017simulator}. Simulator studies demonstrated the value of robotic articulation and visualization, while identifying instrument size as a key constraint \cite{podolsky2017robotic}.
Compact cable-driven 3 mm instruments were subsequently evaluated using reachability analysis and a synthetic cleft palate model. They reduced collisions and visual field obstruction relative to 8 mm instruments \cite{maguire2025}. The present study evaluated simulated repair in living porcine tissue.

\section*{Discussion}
While the 3 mm instruments occupied less of the visual surgical field, suture placement and procedure durations were shorter with the 8 mm instruments. Timing differences in the general procedures can be largely attributed to the variability in manufacturing and assembly of the tools. The 3 mm tools take significantly longer to change out and are more prone to failure due to their construction rather than their function. These times are represented in dissection, interval between procedure stages, and in some cases the suturing. Motion with the 3 mm instruments was also performed more carefully to limit risk of damage to the prototype tools. Learning curve effects, repair complexity, and stitch counts may further explain timing differences between tool sizes. 

While camera distance and specific operative steps varied between trials, the effectiveness and benefits of smaller tools can still be evaluated qualitatively. The image-area comparison reflects the recorded operative view as deemed appropriate by the surgeon for the particular tools and stage of operation. Standardized camera and tool placement relative to the porcine model would not accurately represent the best operating conditions for a surgeon given variation in length, oral cavity size, and hand-eye coordination of the robot and the operator.

Moreover, the standard dVRK provides no force feedback and tissue forces were not directly measured. Wrist friction varies with pitch and cable tension and as a result a direct relationship between robot joint torques and tissue force or injury cannot be established \cite{ho2024friction}. The surgeon reported propensity to tearing of the mucosa during grasping at the defect edge in the 8 mm tool group due to the end effector design not being appropriate for this particular procedure. Additionally, tension relief through lateral relaxing incisions was not performed, which could aid in preventing excessive gripping force on the artificially created mucosal edge \cite{naidu2022cleft}. The 3 mm tools, and in particular robotic wristed elevator, resembled instruments used in conventional cleft repair. The surgeon reported that the purpose-built tools are sensible for translating traditional intervention techniques to the robot-assisted environment. Despite larger shaft diameters, the 8 mm micro-tipped forceps and Cichon graspers had smaller jaw end effectors, facilitating easier knot throws. Their smoother jaw surfaces also appeared to reduce the chance of the suture being caught on the tool itself.

The use of surgically created palate defects in the porcine models limits extrapolation to infant anatomy, healing, and speech and aesthetic outcomes. However, this experiment validates prior work only involving silicone simulators or mathematical analysis \cite{podolsky2017simulator}\cite{maguire2025}. \verifytext{\textbf{[Statement on quality of repair, closure, etc being identical across tool groups according to expert analysis].}} Therefore, the use of the novel miniaturized tools in a simulated porcine repair provides researchers the information necessary to progress the instruments from engineering design towards clinical viability \cite{nasa2016systems}. This study also provides wisdom on robot-assisted procedure planning, as certain surgical subtasks did not require robotic dexterity. In particular, assistant-driven suction and needle extraction is not restricted by instrument shafts remaining inside the oral cavity. Initial and potential relief incisions by scalpel can also remain manual as they do not require the dexterity provided by robotic tools. Introduction of a tool change for suction or cutting would increase procedure duration and workflow complexity and addition of a third or fourth arm would obstruct a surgical assistant's access for tasks that require external intervention.

\verifytext{The findings identify a direct improvement in visualization and instrument handling trade-offs relevant to robot-assisted surgery in confined spaces. Furthermore, the experiments demonstrate successful translation and integration of surgical robotics technology under in vivo conditions. These findings inform future miniaturized tool development and iteration while setting the stage for other clinical use cases. Cleft repair is only one intervention where miniaturized and dexterous tools can empower a surgeon to better treat patients.}
