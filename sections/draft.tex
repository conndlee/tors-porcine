\section{Miniature Instruments for Simulated Cleft Palate Repair}
Cleft palate repair requires tissue dissection and suturing in a restricted oral workspace. Robotic visualization and wrist articulation may assist these tasks, although large instruments can obstruct the view and contact adjacent structures. Maguire and colleagues developed compact, cable-driven, pin-jointed 3 mm instruments for the da Vinci Research Kit and evaluated them using a high-fidelity synthetic cleft palate simulator. Their study combined geometric reachability analysis with physical experiments and reported fewer collisions and reduced image obstruction compared with 8 mm instruments.\supercite{maguire2025}

The present study applied these miniature instruments to simulated cleft palate repair in a live porcine model. The shared tool concept motivated this study, which examined completion and quality of repair in living tissue together with practical measures of visualization and suturing performance. \section{Discussion}
The clinical motivation is to make dexterous transoral reconstruction practical in a small workspace while retaining sufficient capability for dissection and closure. The earlier simulator investigation establishes the engineering rationale; this porcine study addressed a subsequent preclinical question about operative performance in living tissue.\supercite{maguire2025} \textbf{[Interpret the observed completion and closure findings alongside the measured differences in visualization, speed, and tissue interaction.]}

Image-area obstruction offers a practical view of instrument interference with the operative field. Its interpretation requires comparable camera position, magnification, task, and image region. Camera calibration was unavailable, so physical bite depth and spacing require an independent scale or measurement method. Absolute three-dimensional tissue reconstruction cannot be assumed from stereo recordings alone.

Logged joint effort is not a direct measurement of tissue force. Any force estimate requires verification of topic contents, units, instrument-specific transmission, friction, and calibration. Jaw effort describes a different interaction from needle-driving or traction force. Without validation, effort should be reported as a proxy; video deformation alone cannot establish lower force or reduced tissue injury.

This is a small, single-surgeon series. Repeated sutures within a repair must not be treated as independent animals, and a surgeon random effect cannot be estimated from one operator. Trial order, learning, anatomy, and setup may influence comparisons. Similar closure scores alone would not establish equivalence. Porcine simulated defects also limit inference about infant anatomy, healing, speech, and clinical safety. \textbf{[Insert the conclusion supported by the observed technical feasibility and operative differences.]}
